THESIS ABSTRACT  
 

Monitoring is crucial for conservation, but elusive species, including some species of coastal 

delphinids, represent particular challenges. Traditional visual surveys can often underestimate 

metrics such as abundance. For Indian Ocean humpback dolphins (Sousa plumbea) in South 

Africa, near-shore habitats and low encounter rates hinder photographic data collection. Passive 

acoustic monitoring (PAM) offers a non-invasive alternative for species monitoring. This thesis 

investigates the whistling behaviour of S. plumbea in South Africa, focusing on exploring the use 

of signature whistles for individual acoustic detection. Using boat-based recordings (2016–2023) 

from five sites throughout their South African range, it examines whistle variation across 

different geographic scales (including a comparison of whistle-contour time-frequency 

parameters across the extremes of the entire geographic range of the species) and the effect of 

group composition and behaviour on signature whistle production rates, aiming to optimise 

passive acoustic detection methods. The efficacy of individual acoustic detection using PAM was 

directly compared to visual detection (Photo ID) through a two-week survey period in Mossel 

Bay. Following this, an extended acoustic monitoring period along a portion of the south coast 

subpopulation investigated individual acoustic monitoring across three sites, using five 

hydrophones over six weeks, providing abundance estimates, as well as density estimates 

generated using spatially explicit capture-recapture (SECR).  Macrogeographic variation in 

whistle contours was evident between South Africa and India, two populations at the furthest 

extent of the species' geographic range (Random Forest model correct classification rate; South 

Africa 84% (35.2% chance) and India 93% (64.8% chance)). Moderate variation within South 

Africa ranged from 39–90% across study sites, indicating increased acoustic variation with 

geographic distance.  Group composition factors such as group size, group spread, calf presence, 

and behaviour had little impact on signature whistle production. These findings suggest that 

these factors are not crucial for optimising the detection of signature whistles in the acoustic 

monitoring of S. plumbea. Comparing monitoring methods, photographic identification was more 

cost-effective (6,400 ZAR.dolphin-1) than acoustic methods (12,266.40 ZAR.dolphin-1). However, 

acoustic detection was diurnal, identifying more individuals and signature whistles at night and 

across the entire survey area. Visual surveys detected dolphins at only two locations, emphasising 

PAM's effectiveness as an alternative method for detecting S. plumbea. Abundance estimates from 

signature whistle detected along a 120 km stretch of South Africa's south coast revealed limited 

individual movement, with low abundance (SECR: 23–26; POPAN: 26, 95% CI 23–30) and density 

(0.000061–0.000439 animals.ha-1). This proof-of-concept study explored mooring configurations 

and model optimisation, demonstrating that expanding the geographic range of the acoustic array 

could be more effective for increasing unique recaptures than clustering acoustic receivers in a 

smaller area. This thesis demonstrates that PAM is a reliable alternative to photographic surveys 

to study the elusive coastal species S. plumbea and produces sufficient data to understand 

population abundances and movements using individual acoustic signatures. These techniques 

offer a scalable and reproducible approach to producing population estimates, which can equip 

wildlife practitioners and scientists with the tools to monitor this elusive species reliably over 

time and space.

1 GENERAL INTRODUCTION 

ACOUSTIC COMMUNICATION IN DELPHINIDS  
 

Communication is the transfer of information from signaller to receiver and can occur through 

visual, olfactory, chemical, acoustic senses, or, at very short distances, through touch (Labra et al., 

1999; Marler, 1961). The complexity of a species' communication system is often linked to its 

level of social organisation, and understanding how species communicate can provide 

information on the species' relationship both to its conspecifics and environment (Klump, 2020). 

Signals can contain information on innate or fixed aspects such as size, age, and sex (Seyfarth et 

al., 2010) or variable aspects such as health, arousal, and rank (Bergman & Sheehan, 2013; 

Briefer, 2020; Taylor et al., 2010). The ability to recognise other species, groups or even specific 

individuals within the group can be indicative of social complexity and sophisticated 

communication processes within a species (Yorzinski, 2017).  Animals can prioritise a certain 

type of communication system based on circumstance, such as proximity to conspecifics, or 

environmental conditions, such as aquatic or high-density habitats (Ey & Fischer, 2009). In such 

environments, acoustic signalling is beneficial due to the flexibility in the rapid transmission of 

signals and effective propagation over varied distances (Ladich & Winkler, 2017). 

 

Delphinids, a family of over 35 species, have a range of social structures from large pods of 1000s 

of individuals with continuous fission-fusion dynamics (Richards et al., 2013) to strong 

matrilineal groups (Deecke et al., 2010) and are present throughout the global oceans (Best, 

2007) leading to the development of a sophisticated acoustic repertoire (Janik, 2009; Luís et al., 

2021). Delphinids can produce two main types of sound: clicks (including burst pulse sounds) 

and tonal frequency-modulated sounds (including low-frequency narrow-band sounds, LFNs, 

and whistles) (Jones et al., 2020; Luís et al., 2021; Popper, 1980). Clicks are short signals that 

reach into the ultrasonic frequency range and occur in trains with an inter-click interval of more 

than 10 ms (Au et al., 1974; Jones et al., 2020). They are most commonly used in echolocation to 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



17 
 

perceive the environment, especially during foraging (Herman & Tavolga, 1980). Burst pulse 

signals are rapid click trains, typically defined as having an inter-click interval of less than 10 ms 

(Luís et al., 2021). The frequency of click trains and burst pulses fluctuates depending on function 

and behavioural context. Burst pulses appear to be involved in communication interactions 

(Lammers 2003), as the inter-click interval exceeds the round-trip propagation time required to 

receive individual click echoes (Ivanov, 2004). In a review of sounds produced by bottlenose 

dolphins (Tursiops truncatus), Jones et al., (2020) describe low-frequency narrow band (LFN) 

sounds as having a fundamental frequency of less than 2 kHz (0.26 – 1.28 kHz) and durations less 

than 0.41 s (0.01 – 0.41 seconds). LFNs are rarely reported within the literature, and it is difficult 

to determine how widely used these sounds are (Jones et al., 2020). 

 

Whistles are narrowband frequency-modulated sounds and one of the more commonly studied 

call types due to their dominant use by many species and ease of measurement (Herzing, 2014; 

Janik, 2009). Most whistling delphinid species focus their energy below 20 kHz (Popper, 1980), 

yet the maximum recorded fundamental frequencies can reach over 40 kHz in T. truncatus (41.80 

kHz: Hiley et al., 2017). Whistles function as a communicative signal, and they are commonly 

produced under socialising contexts (La Manna et al., 2019; Marley et al., 2017; Papale et al., 

2017). Whistles can convey arousal states and behaviour (Quick & Janik, 2008) and also function 

to convey identity (Sayigh et al., 1999) within the frequency modulation of the tonal sound 

(Kershenbaum et al., 2013; Sayigh et al., 1999). The fission-fusion group dynamics of delphinid 

species, such as common (Delphinus spp.) or bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops spp.), lead to a greater 

level of individual distinctiveness within their whistle repertoire to account for the fluidity of 

these associations and ensure the maintenance of complex social relationships and behaviour 

(Fearey et al., 2019; Reiss et al., 1997).

SIGNATURE WHISTLES IN DELPHINIDS 
 

Individual recognition in animals occurs through multiple senses in different species. However, 

in the marine environment, communication through acoustic channels is the most powerful and 

widespread (Ladich & Winkler, 2017). Unlike voice cues, which can affect all animal calls, 

bottlenose dolphins can encode individual identity information within the frequency modulation 

pattern of whistles (Janik & Slater, 1997; Sayigh et al., 2017). These individually unique whistles 

are termed ‘signature whistles’ (Janik, 2009). Caldwell and Caldwell (1965) first identified these 

individually distinctive frequency contours in bottlenose dolphins and hypothesised that these 

were used to transmit identity information. A signature whistle has since been defined more 

formally as a learned, individually distinctive whistle type in a dolphin’s repertoire that 

broadcasts the identity of the whistle owner as a contact call (Sayigh et al., 2007). Individually 

distinct calls are extremely rare in the animal kingdom (Boughman & Moss, 2006). Signature 

whistles develop in the first year of a calf's life through vocal learning and the auditory experience 

of the environment and conspecifics therein (Sayigh et al., 1990). MacFarlane (2016) identified 

three potential functions for signature whistles: maintaining contact, regaining lost contact, and 

advertising identity. As signature whistles are developed through vocal learning, this ability 

means that whistle matching is also possible  (Janik, 2000). Individuals are known to whistle 

match and copy the signature whistles of those with whom they form a strong social bond (King 

et al., 2014; Probert et al., 2021). However, this copying occurs in small numbers and usually 

when the copied individual is present (King et al., 2013), with the copied whistles identifiable 

through parameter variations within the copy (King et al., 2013).  

 

Research on signature whistles has been conducted for over 60 years (Janik & Sayigh, 2013), 

mainly in T. truncatus, using either captive (Janik & Slater, 1998) or temporarily restrained wild 

individuals (Esch et al., 2009; Sayigh et al., 1990, 1999). As signature whistles act predominantly 

as cohesion calls, they can constitute up to 100% of whistles produced during these separation 

events (when isolated or restrained from conspecifics) (Janik & Slater, 1998). However, this value 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



19 
 

is considerably reduced in recordings from non-restrained wild populations (38% - 70% (Cook 

et al., 2004). Functioning primarily as contact calls in these freely interacting contexts, signature 

whistles are produced at higher rates when animals meet at sea (Quick & Janik, 2008), mother 

and calves reunite (Smolker et al., 1993) and when engaged in certain behaviours, such as 

socialising, that require a form of individual recognition (Sayigh et al., 1999). In bottlenose 

dolphins, these contact calls were identified as bouts of stereotyped whistles emitted within 1 to 

10 seconds of each other (Janik, 2013). Although most widely documented in bottlenose dolphins, 

signature whistles have been reported in other odontocete species, such as narwhals (Monodon 

monoceros) (Shapiro, 2006), short-finned pilot whales (Globicephala macrorhynchus) (Sayigh et 

al., 2013), melon-headed whales (Peponocephala electra) (Kaplan et al., 2014) and common 

dolphins (Delphinus delphis) (Fearey et al., 2019). Some studies have also documented likely 

signature whistle use within the genus Sousa using opportunistic recording events (Cheng et al., 

2017; Van Parijs & Corkeron, 2001a).

PRACTICAL USES OF SIGNATURE WHISTLES FOR RESEARCH  
 

Research approaches using individual identification (capture-recapture) can provide long-term 

metrics, from the population level down to the individual, such as abundance, habitat range or 

movement, social networks, and group size (Hammond, 1986; Karlsson et al., 2005). Individual 

identification from naturally occurring markings is useful when more invasive marking 

techniques are challenging to perform, detect, or are ethically inappropriate (Terry et al., 2005). 

Furthermore, individually distinctive acoustic signals are less invasive and have been suggested 

as a suitable alternative to active marking or photographic identification (Janik et al., 2013). 

Signature whistles are naturally occurring, individually distinctive and stable over time (up to 43 

years, Sayigh et al., 2022) and overcome many of the biases inherent in using artificial marks or 

marks from scarring or injuries (James et al., 2022). Although previously suggested by Janik et al., 

(2013), Longden et al., (2020) first identified signature whistles as a potentially suitable 

technique to generate abundance estimates in a Namibian population of bottlenose dolphins 

(Tursiops truncatus) by using signature whistles in a capture-recapture framework where the 

results compared favourably with established photographic methods (Friday et al., 2008; Urian 

et al., 2015) . Further work has also investigated the role of recording duty cycles and fine-scale 

hydrophone placement on the use of signature whistles in a capture-recapture framework 

(Fearey et al., 2022). Forming part of the SWORD project (Signature Whistles for Occurrence, 

Recapture and Density, Fearey et al., 2021), this proposed methodology, using signature whistles 

in a capture-recapture framework for acoustic-based monitoring, has yet to be applied outside 

the genus Tursiops.

ACOUSTIC BEHAVIOUR OF SOUSA SPP 
 
There are around 50 studies on the acoustic behaviour across all four species in the genus Sousa, 

with Sousa chinensis the most widely published, around Malaysia and China (Hoffman et al., 2015; 

Sims et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013), then Sousa sahulensis in Australia (de Freitas et al., 2015; 

Hoffman et al., 2017; Schultz & Corkeron, 1994a; Van Parijs & Corkeron, 2001b), as well as a single 

publication on the acoustic behaviour of the IUCN Critically Endangered Sousa teuszii in western 

Africa (Weir, 2010) (Table 1.1). S. chinensis has the most widely defined whistle repertoire with 

maximum reported whistle frequencies of 33 kHz (Wang et al., 2013). Whistle measurements 

vary across studies, with some studies presenting limited information (Sims et al., 2012; Zbinden 

et al., 1977) and some are limited by the bandwidth of recording equipment (Schultz & Corkeron, 

1994a; Van Parijs & Corkeron, 2001b; Weir, 2010). 

 

To date, three published studies have examined the acoustic characteristics of Sousa plumbea in 

Pakistan, India and Zanzibar. Zbinden et al., (1977) published the first acoustic study on S. 

chinensis (now S. plumbea) from the Indus River delta region, Pakistan. The study was limited by 

equipment frequency range but demonstrated a long whistle duration of up to 2.5 seconds and 

call combinations of burst pulse trains and whistle production (screams). Nearly four decades 

later, Bopardikar et al., (2018) published a full description of the whistle repertoire (single 

element tonal signals) for S. plumbea along the Maharashtra coast, India, with a frequency range 

of up to 33 kHz and durations of up to 1.6 seconds. The first click analysis for the genus was 

published by Yang et al., (2020), who showed a high rate of species classification of S. plumbea 

compared to sympatric T. aduncus. Across all studies, caution should be taken in extrapolating 

findings to other S. plumbea populations, as interspecific and intraspecific differences in time 

frequency features across regions have been documented in Sousa spp. (Hoffman et al., 2015; 

Wang et al., 2013).

THE STUDY SPECIES: SOUSA PLUMBEA 
 

Sousa plumbea was recognised as a distinct species from S. chinensis in 2014 based on molecular 

analysis, skeletal morphology, external morphology and colouration (Jefferson & Rosenbaum, 

2014; Mendez et al., 2013). The range of S. plumbea extends from their most southwestern point 

in False Bay, South Africa, along the southern and eastern African coastlines to as far as the west 

coast of India (Plön et al., 2015). In the southern African subregion, S. plumbea has been recorded 

from South Africa, Mozambique, Tanzania (including Zanzibar), Kenya, the Comoro Islands and 

Madagascar (Best, 2007). 

 

Globally, S. plumbea is not highly abundant anywhere in its range, with global abundance likely in 

the low ten thousands (Braulik et al., 2015). Previous research in East Africa on S. plumbea has 

shown small, declining populations from several localised studies but a region wide assessment 

has not been done. Research in Maputo Bay and Bazaruto Bay, southern Mozambique, indicated 

low densities of 0.47 dolphins per km2 (Peddemors, 1999) and a population in the Maputo Bay 

area of ~105 individuals in the late 1990s (Guissamulo & Cockcroft, 2005). In Zanzibar, further 

along the east coast of Africa, there has been a 63% reduction in the population size since 2002, 

with the last population abundance estimate of 19 (95% CI 14-25) in the Menai Bay conservation 

area in 2015 (Sharpe & Berggren, 2019). Limited information on population density is available 

across the rest of their African range, especially at a broader scale (Braulik et al., 2015). In South 

Africa, group size in S. plumbea is typically small, as previous studies have only seen a maximum 

of 30 individuals in a group, but groups are more commonly less than 10 (Plön et al., 2015). 

Several population estimates have been made throughout their South Africa range, mostly in 

localised areas such as in one or two bays, and from ~ 20 to 96 individuals (Table 1.2). A collection 

of photo identification catalogues from previous studies collected around South Africa from 2000 

to 2016 identified 247 unique individuals along the whole South African distribution, suggesting 

a total South African population of fewer than 500 animals (Vermeulen et al., 2018), substantially 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



27 
 

lower than the 1000 estimated as the total country population by Karczmarski (1996) from photo 

ID data available in the late 1990s. 

 

The general life history of S. plumbea in South Africa reflects that of a medium-sized coastal 

delphinid that occupies a very narrow habitat niche, typically less than 3 km from shore and in 

waters shallower than 25 m. Populations occur around a variety of coastal habitats, including 

rocky reefs, coastal lagoons, and shallow, protected bays (Braulik et al., 2015). Diet records for S. 

plumbea have been mainly restricted to specimens accidentally caught in shark nets along the 

Kwazulu-Natal (KZN) east coast, with little data available for the south coast region. The few 

records on their diet show them feeding mainly on estuarine and demersal fish (Peddemors, 

1999). Their coastal habitat influences the feeding methods they adopt. This habitat means they 

are often associated with large estuarine systems and harbours. Although considered a marine 

dolphin species, there are a few reports of dolphins entering river systems, travelling up to 1 km 

within the Swartkops and Bushmans rivers in the Eastern Cape and into the Knysna estuary in 

the Western Cape (Plön et al., 2015). These sightings are often associated with brackish effluent 

in the lower estuarine environment during high tides. However, in the Breede River, Western 

Cape, there have been confirmed sightings and reports of S. plumbea as far as 20 km upriver (Sea 

Search, unpublished data).  

 

MOVEMENT OF SOUSA PLUMBEA  
 

Sousa plumbea are the most threatened marine mammal species in South Africa (Plön et al., 2015), 

making it essential for researchers to conduct comprehensive and effective long-term 

conservation assessments. Understanding the degree of dispersal and individual movement can 

highlight ecologically sensitive areas that require protection (Gormley et al., 2012). Previous 

photographic capture-recapture studies that assessed residency patterns or movement have 

largely focused on discrete regional areas around South Africa, which have been inadequate to 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



28 
 

provide a comprehensive assessment of the entire population (Bouveroux et al., 2018; James et 

al., 2015; Vermeulen et al., 2018) (Table 1.2). In all these assessments, connectivity between 

locations was poorly understood based on the limited data available (Durham, 1994; Jobson, 

2006; Karczmarski et al., 1999; Keith et al., 2013; Saayman & Tayler, 1973). 

 

In South Africa, S. plumbea is thought to be split into two distinct populations, one off the south 

coast and one off the east coast (Figure 1.1). The connectivity between these two regions is 

minimal, as indicated by low encounter rates (Durham, 1994; Keith et al., 2002), few strandings 

(Findlay et al., 1992), limited sightings during aerial surveys (Cockcroft et al., 1989), and 

infrequent captures in shark nets along southern KZN and the Transkei region (Atkins et al., 

2013). This limited connectivity is further supported by the absence of matches in photographic 

identification catalogues between Algoa Bay and KZN, regions approximately 1050 km apart, 

reinforcing the existence of two geographically distinct populations (Karczmarski et al., 1999; 

Vermeulen et al., 2018). The south coast population is less well studied, with most information 

available from studies within specific bays, notably Algoa Bay, which was well studied in the early 

1990s and again 16 years later, showing significant decreases in sightings rates, group sizes, the 

number of overall identified animals and an increase in the number of solitary animals 

(Bouveroux et al., 2018; Karczmarski et al., 1999; Karczmarski & Cockcroft, 1998; Koper et al., 

2016). A series of student projects in Plettenberg Bay described similar trends throughout the 

region (Conry, 2017; Greenwood, 2013; Jobson, 2006) as well as Bouveroux et al.,’s (2019) study 

on social networks and some early shore-based observations throughout the southeastern 

coastline (Saayman & Tayler, 1973; Saayman, 1972). West of Plettenberg Bay, only a single 

published estimate exists on population size in Mossel Bay (James et al., 2015) and a general 

description of sighting rates and seasonality from whale-watching data (Vinding, 2016). 

 

Only one published paper has studied the movement of S. plumbea individuals at a national scale 

along the South African coastline using available photo ID data from 2000-2016 (Vermeulen et

al., 2018). The study found the average number of re-sightings of individuals across this period 

was six for the whole distribution, decreasing to 3.3 for individuals occurring in the south coast 

population only. Median range estimates for individuals were 120 km, with an overall range of 

movement of 30 km to 500 km, but most movements were less than 200 km suggesting that this 

is a typical home range or along-shore range. The study highlighted general 'hotspots' for the 

species along the coastline. However, it indicated that, due to their reported range distance, the 

protection of existing discrete marine protected areas (MPAs) may not be sufficient to protect 

this species.

SOCIALITY IN SOUSA PLUMBEA 
 

Group structures within cetaceans range from stable matriarchal family pods, as seen in orcas 

(Orcinus orca) (Deecke et al., 2010), to the variable and complex fission-fusion groups of 

bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops spp.), where group membership can change every 10-20 mins 

(Connor & Krützen, 2015; Galezo et al., 2018). The drivers of fission-fusion assortment are based 

on the benefits and constraints of the group over time and space (Aureli et al., 2008). However, 

group structure within this is far more complex, and it is essential to understand the variability 

in individual relationships within a group, which are key to social complexity (Hunt et al., 2019). 

It has been shown that the same individuals can display different social structures in different 

conditions, for example, when they compete to increase their reproductive success (Gowans, 

2019). Aureli et al., (2008) demonstrated that individual variation in habitat, affiliations and 

group size are essential factors to consider in assessing the social structure of a population.  

 

In S. plumbea, there has been relatively little published research regarding their social 

organisation (Bouveroux et al., 2019; Karczmarski et al., 1999; Saayman & Tayler, 1973). In South 

Africa, only 10s of animals have been identified a handful of times each in previous studies 

(Bouveroux et al., 2019; James et al., 2015; Koper et al., 2016; Vermeulen et al., 2018) (Table 1.2). 

In Algoa Bay S. plumbea had social bonds that were fluid and mostly of short-duration affiliations 

(Karczmarski et al., 1999). A more recent study in the Plettenberg Bay area (Bouveroux et al., 

2019), detected two occurrences of stronger pairwise affiliations. Bouveroux et al., (2019) 

demonstrated a generally low level of gregariousness, with a well-differentiated social network 

with half the sampled dolphins (14 of 29 seen three or more times) showing strong social bonds 

between individuals, mostly as pairs. They suggest that the higher social bonds seen in this study 

could be a result of the decreasing group sizes and overall population in the area.  

 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



32 
 

Along the south coast of South Africa, a substantial decrease in the average group size of S. 

plumbea has been observed in several locations. For example, in Plettenberg Bay, average group 

sizes dropped from seven to four (Conry, 2017; Jobson, 2006), while in Algoa Bay, the average 

group size dropped from seven to three (Bouveroux et al., 2018; Karczmarski et al., 1999). 

General sociality in S. plumbea in South Africa is thought to be reflective of their small group sizes 

and an increase in the number of solitary individuals (Koper et al., 2016), limiting the number of 

potential associations (Gowans et al., 2007). 

 

CURRENT CONSERVATION STATUS OF SOUSA PLUMBEA IN SOUTH AFRICA 
 

Research on S. plumbea in South Africa since the late 1960s (see review by Elwen et al., 2011) has 

covered a wide range of topics, including socio-ecology (Karczmarski et al., 1999; Koper et al., 

2016; Saayman, 1972), diet (Barros & Cockroft, 1999), anatomy (Frainer et al., 2019; Plön et al., 

2012), regional distribution, habitat use, and movements (Atkins et al., 2005; Bouveroux et al., 

2018; Koper et al., 2016; Vermeulen et al., 2018), local population abundance estimates (James et 

al., 2015; Keith et al., 2002), vulnerability to direct human impacts such as bycatch in shark nets 

(Atkins et al., 2013, 2016; Cockcroft, 1990, 1994), vessel traffic (Karczmarski et al., 1997; Koper 

et al., 2016) and health assessment, and pollutant loads (Aznar-Alemany et al., 2019; Cockcroft, 

1999; Gui et al., 2016; Lane et al., 2014). However, these regional studies are limited in their 

capacity to provide insights into broader population health and dynamics. These studies have 

provided crucial baseline information and identified potential threats to the species. Still, they 

lack key information, including a reliable national abundance estimate, which is necessary to 

document future population trends.  

 

In South Africa, although no empirical data are available on population trends at a broader 

national scale, Koper et al., (2016) and Bouveroux et al., (2018) suggested a significant decline in 

the frequency of occurrence, average group size, number of calves and the number of identified 

https://scholar.sun.ac.za



33 
 

individuals when compared to a similar study in the early 1990s in Algoa Bay (Karczmarski et al., 

1999). For Plettenberg Bay, a decrease in population size of 50% between 2002 and 2012 has 

been suggested, although this was based on limited sampling effort (Greenwood, 2013). In 

Richard's Bay, shark net mortalities have contributed to an unsustainable loss of S. plumbea (4.3 

individuals or 5% - 10% of the population per year; (Atkins et al., 2013, 2016). These data 

strongly indicate an overall population decline along South Africa’s coastline. Considering these 

inferred population declines, S. plumbea was up-listed to “Endangered” in South Africa on the 

IUCN Red List (Plön et al., 2015). This was based on an estimated extent of occurrence of < 5,000 

km², a continuing decline in habitat quality (and likely area of occupancy) in key resource areas 

correlating with coastal development, the fragmentation of populations, and continuing adult 

mortality from anthropogenic disturbances (especially shark-nets; Plön et al., 2015). A quarter of 

the photographed South African population has been recorded with injured or abnormal 

rostrums, possibly caused by either fishing line entanglements, extreme feeding behaviours or 

genetic depression, leading to apparent conditions such as broken or misaligned jaws (Frainer et 

al., 2023). Most observed individuals were adults; therefore, it is difficult to know the extent of 

such impact on the population, as it is unknown how many juveniles did not survive these injuries 

or abnormalities. However, the wide prevalence throughout the South African range is likely 

driven by their small, fragmented population (Frainer et al., 2023).  

 

Overall, there are limited population estimates throughout the species' full South African range. 

As a result, population trends across their range must often be inferred by assessing the extent of 

mortality or the degree of decline in relative sighting rates or group sizes. However, where data 

are available to evaluate changes over time, there are clear indications of significant declines in 

abundance, relative sighting rates, and/or group sizes. The reported levels of threats due to their 

restricted habitat and high reported mortality in portions of their range means that S. plumbea 

population size is almost definitely decreasing in South Africa (Braulik et al., 2015).

STUDY LOCATION: SOUTH AFRICA  
 

South Africa has a long, diverse coastline spanning 2,798 km, from rocky reefs to sandy beaches 

and estuaries. Mixing between the warmer Agulhas current and the cool Benguela current creates 

dynamic ocean currents, causing highly fluctuating water temperatures. The most westerly range 

of S. plumbea is at False Bay, at the most south-westerly tip of the continent, with no range 

extension into the westerly cooler Benguela region. The mean water temperatures along S. 

plumbea’s coastal distribution range from 19 °C – 26 °C in the northeast, while the cooler south 

coast region has mean water temperatures of 14 °C – 20 °C (Dallas, 2008). Several major estuaries 

occur along the coastline, with many under regular management (Whitfield et al., 2012). Estuaries 

have been shown to be a likely habitat for the species, overlapping with many anthropogenic 

impacts, with some individuals being seen upriver in brackish, estuarine regions (Plön et al., 

2015). South Africa’s coastline is highly developed, with the vast majority of the coastline affected 

by human structures, including large coastal developments, active harbours, urbanisation, and 

development in estuaries (Claassens et al., 2023). However, a significant section of the S. plumbea 

range in the Western Cape falls within protected areas such as De Hoop, Stilbaai, Goukamma, 

Robberg, and Tsikamma marine protected areas (Mann-Lang et al., 2021) (Figure 1.1).

THE SOUSA CONSORTIUM 
 

A lack of scientific knowledge has been recognised as a major threat to S. plumbea conservation 

actions in South Africa, with many aspects of the species’ life history and current changes to its 

population poorly understood. Given the lack of data on this species, a nationwide research 

collaboration, the SouSA consortium, was established in May 2016 (Vermeulen et al., 2018). The 

consortium aims to address the need for more knowledge on the conservation biology of S. 

plumbea in South Africa nationally and work as a collective group to achieve these aims. The 

consortium was recognised at the World Marine Mammal Conference in 2019 as a leading 

example in collaboration (S. Dines Judges Discretionary Award) and has collectively published 

several publications on S. plumbea, collating data at a national scale (Frainer et al., 2023; Plön et 

al., 2021; Vermeulen et al., 2018). The SouSA consortium is currently working with public, private 

and international stakeholders, such as the IUCN SOS group, to develop an effective biodiversity 

action plan for S. plumbea in South Africa. In 2024, the Indian Ocean Humpback Dolphin 

Conservation Network (HuDoNET) was established. HuDoNET was modelled on the successful 

initiatives of the Conservation Consortium of the Atlantic humpback dolphin (CCAHD) and the 

SouSA Consortium, with the aim to develop a build connections and collaboration among 

stakeholders throughout the species’ range to encourage coordinated conservation actions 

(Atkins et al., 2024).

THESIS OVERVIEW: CONCEPTUAL THESIS FRAMEWORK  

 

The limitations of visual and olfactory senses in the marine environment have led to the evolution 

of sophisticated acoustic communication systems in cetaceans (Herman & Tavolga, 1980). 

Dolphins are known for their high vocal activity, producing a variety of acoustic signals, including 

frequency-modulated whistles used for communication (Jones et al., 2020). Among these, 

signature whistles are unique signals that are highly stable and stereotyped, serving as 

identification markers and contact calls in specific contexts (Caldwell & Caldwell, 1965; Sayigh et 

al., 2007). Evidence for signature whistle use has been presented across several species of 

odontocetes (see review in Janik, 2009), including preliminary evidence in Sousa spp. (Cheng et 

al., 2017; Gridley et al., Appendix 1; Van Parijs & Corkeron, 2001a). This thesis aims to understand 

the whistling behaviour of S. plumbea in South Africa and explore how signature whistles can be 

effectively used for acoustic monitoring of this elusive coastal species. Here, I present a 

conceptual framework for my thesis (Figure 1.2). The framework describes each data chapter 

(Chapters 2-5) and the main themes within. The figure presents how the chapters are linked, from 

investigating general whistle characteristics and variability across spatial scales in Chapter 2 to a 

focus on the behavioural effects on signature whistle production in Chapter 3. In Chapter 4, the 

use of passive acoustic monitoring to recapture signature whistles is compared to a more 

standard approach of boat-based photo ID over a short field study period. Finally, individual 

detection over time and space for capture-recapture models is investigated in Chapter 5. Using 

signature whistles for occurrence, recapture and density (SWORD, Fearey et al., 2021), the thesis 

provides baseline data on how individual passive acoustic monitoring can best be utilised for this 

threatened species.
